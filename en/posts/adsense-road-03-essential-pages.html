The Road to AdSense (3): The Essential Pages — from About to Privacy Policy

27 October 202617 min read3125 words

In the previous two parts you laid your site's foundations: the niche, domain, and structural decisions made before any content. This part moves to the layer the AdSense review team examines within the first minutes, before reading a single article of yours: the essential pages that tell them this is a real site run by a real person who takes responsibility for what gets published. These pages do not raise your earnings or rank first in search — but their absence or weakness ends applications with record speed.

The rule I want to fix in your mind from the start: the review team does not read your site the way an interested reader does. They run a policy checklist, and its first items are questions like these: who owns this site? How do I reach them? Does the site disclose how it handles visitor data? The essential pages are the pre-written answers to these questions. In this article I will walk through each page: exactly what to write, what not to write, where to place it — then close with a checklist you can apply to your site item by item.

The rule of this part: essential pages are not paperwork stuffed in to pass a review — they are a compact trust contract between you, your visitor, and Google at once.

1) Why pages get checked before articles

Imagine you are a review employee at Google with two applications in front of you. The first: a site with thirty good articles but no contact page, no privacy policy, no mention of an owner anywhere. The second: fifteen articles of the same quality, but the site introduces itself clearly, offers an email for contact, and explains its privacy practices transparently. Which application consumes more of your time while you try to verify its credibility? The answer is obvious — and that is exactly what happens in AdSense review rooms daily.

The official AdSense policies state that a publisher must be able to receive correspondence, and that a site using Google ads must provide clear information about visitor privacy and cookie interactions. These are not professional-courtesy tips — they are written clauses, and violating them is a documented rejection reason that returns in the report as "site information incomplete" or similar. Strong articles do not compensate for a missing contact page, because the clause evaluates the site as a whole, not the content alone.

Then there is the third dimension many overlook: these pages also close a trust gap for the ordinary visitor. The visitor considering subscribing, buying, or even simply returning often looks for an About page to know who is speaking to them. Many sites lose loyal readers simply because their owner looks "anonymous and faceless." In this sense, essential pages are a double investment: they raise review acceptance and improve visitor behavior at the same time.

There are subtle differences separating approved sites from rejected ones even with similar content. The rejected site usually has the pages but in a "checkbox" style: generic texts, no name, no date, links that go nowhere. The approved site writes them as if addressing a real visitor who might become a business partner tomorrow. This difference in intent shows in every line, and the review team — which sees dozens of sites daily — spots it within minutes. Write your pages as if someone important will read them carefully, because someone important will actually read them.

2) The About page: a human voice, not a company

The About page is the page reviewers read most carefully, because it answers the question the content itself cannot: who writes this? A strong page opens with a short, explicit definition: your real or professional name, your actual field of expertise, and the reason you write for this specific site. If you are a developer sharing experience with developers, say so. If you are a teacher sharing lesson plans, say so. The generic page of the "we are a team passionate about quality content" type lowers your site's score rather than raising it, because it is a phrasing anyone could write about any site.

After the opening definition, add the proof layer: what have you actually done in this field? Sites you built, projects you tried, results you can describe without exaggeration. This is where serious sites easily distinguish themselves, because those talking about a field without experience find nothing to write in this paragraph and resort to vague filler phrases. Then add your face photo if you are comfortable with it — photos are not a policy requirement, but they raise human trust noticeably, and can be replaced with a personal logo and a consistent writing style if you prefer not to appear.

Close the page with two bridges: a link to the contact page, and links to your best foundational articles summarizing your approach. This turns About from a static identity sheet into a routing hub distributing the serious visitor to your best work. A practical tip: write it in first person "I" or "we" if it is a team, and do not flip between the two forms within the same page — voice consistency is one of the marks by which an experienced reader identifies a real writer.

3) The Contact page: the clause that never drops

The AdSense policies are explicit on this item: the publisher is responsible for the advertising system on their site, and therefore a clear contact channel must be available to visitors and parties. The contact page fulfills this clause visually and practically: a direct email address, a message form, and ideally a second channel like an active social account. A site that cannot be contacted looks — rightly — like it is hiding something, whether it actually is or not.

Many bloggers postpone this page thinking it is for large companies, then discover at review time that it is a check item with no exceptions. The truth is it is the easiest page you will write on your entire site: an email address, two lines explaining the kind of messages you receive, and a simple form. Also write what you do not receive, clearly — "I do not respond to paid link placement offers," for example. Such candor protects your time and raises the appearance of seriousness to any professional reader.

But candor needs skillful phrasing so it does not sound rude. The successful formula declares in one sentence then opens the door: "I welcome real service inquiries and collaborations, and do not accept paid link or ad-exchange offers." This way the serious party understands the door is open, the annoying one understands their message will not be read, and the reviewer — and the visitor alike — sees that a person who knows what they want stands behind this site. A contact page that defines its boundaries clearly is usually the first professional mark a reader touches on the whole site.

A good contact form has three essential fields, no more: name, email, message. Every additional field lowers completion rates. If you receive service requests, add a purpose dropdown, but do not turn the form into a survey. After the fields, a real status message must appear: if the message was delivered, a clear confirmation; if sending failed, alternatives like direct email. The form that always says "sent" even when it fails is a real source of visitor complaints — your site's reputation is built on these small details before the big topics.

More important than the design itself: the promises on the page must match reality. If you write "I reply within 24 hours," be ready to do so, or delete the timeframe and write "I personally reply to serious messages." The reviewer does not test your inbox, but the visitor actually does, and the disappointed visitor becomes — without exception — a permanent critic. Keep promises realistic and execution faster than the promise; that formula wins in every scenario.

4) The Privacy Policy: what you must name explicitly

The privacy policy is the only page on this list that the official Google ads policies mention in explicit text: any site using AdSense must display a privacy policy consistent with the publisher program policies. The core points this page covers are five: what data your site collects, how it uses it, who it shares it with, how the visitor manages their choices, and how to contact you about it. You need no legal background to write it — you need honesty and completeness.

The most important paragraph — the one rejected sites omit — is the third-party ads paragraph. Name explicitly that your site uses Google ads, that Google as a service provider uses cookies to show ads based on previous visits to this site or other sites, and that the user can disable personalized ads from Google's ads settings. This phrasing is not a luxury: the policies require clear disclosure of third-party cookie usage, and many "privacy policy insufficient" rejections trace back to the absence of this exact paragraph.

After it, add paragraphs on what you yourself collect: form data the visitor enters willingly, and anonymous usage data if you use analytics tools. Then mention the visitor's rights to delete their data or contact you about it, and the page's last-update date. Write in clear, direct language without pretentious legal jargon — the goal is for the ordinary reader to understand what happens to their data, not to dazzle them with sentences they cannot parse. A good privacy page can be read in three minutes and covers everything above without padding.

If your audience includes visitors from the EU or the UK, add a paragraph about cookie consent: that the site shows a consent banner or gives the user a choice before loading personalized ads. AdSense has offered its official consent message tool for years now, and clearly stating in your page that you use it — or plan to — puts you on the safe side. Do not worry if you do not understand these tools' details yet; we will cover them fully in the policies part. What is required today is that the paragraph exists and is honest about what your site actually does.

5) Terms and Disclaimer: two pages that protect you

The Terms of Use page sets the rules of the game between you and your visitor: what may be done with your content, liability limits, your linking policy, and comment rules if enabled. It is not required in every AdSense case, but its presence raises the site's wall in review and pre-solves future disputes with visitors who copied your content or used it in ways you do not accept. Write it as ten to fifteen short paragraphs covering these points, without fake legal pomp.

The disclaimer page gains doubled importance in money, health, and education topics. Its core message is one: the content here is for education and information, it is not individual professional advice, and decisions the reader makes based on it are their own responsibility within their own circumstances. In the online-income field specifically, an honest disclaimer that says "I do not guarantee you my exact results because our circumstances differ" protects your site, preserves your reputation, and aligns perfectly with Google's principle against unrealistic gain promises.

Both pages are written once and updated on meaningful change. A visible update date at the top of each page is a life mark that makes reviewer and visitor alike trust that what they are reading is not a template text copied years ago. Later in the series' policies part we will revisit these two pages from the ad-compliance angle in detail — here the goal is knowing that their complete, honest presence is part of the foundation wall everything else stands on.

6) Where to place the pages: footer and menu

A good site makes these pages discoverable within two seconds from any page. The conventional placement is the footer strip: "About," "Contact," "Privacy Policy," "Terms," and "Disclosure" arranged in one clear column or row. This is the position reviewers check first because it is the recognized standard, and hiding them in links buried inside texts or a distant archive page counts as practical absence even if technically present.

The main menu at the top carries navigation links only: Home, Articles, Categories, About, and the "Request a service" button if you offer one. Do not put the Privacy Policy in the top menu — it is not a navigation destination but a reference document, and its natural place is the footer. Balance matters here: a clean navigation menu serves reading, and a complete footer serves trust and requirements. This split is what major sites follow, and it is what the reviewer will sense as a sign of an organized site.

And do not forget the second language if your site is bilingual. Every essential page must have a counterpart in every language your site displays, and the reviewer who enters the English version and finds an untranslated "Privacy Policy" will count it as an incomplete-site signal. If you build an Arabic-only blog there is no issue — but if you have an English version, the rule is: everything existing in Arabic has an English equivalent, from the first article to the last footer item.

7) Common mistakes that get pages rejected

The first and most common mistake: pages that exist formally but are empty in substance. A privacy policy copied from a foreign template that does not mention your site's name or AdSense, an About page with a generic line repeated across thousands of sites, a contact form that sends nothing. The seasoned reviewer opens these pages and actually reads them, and the copied template shows from the first paragraph because it contains no detail specific to your site. A page written about your site, even in simple style, beats the elegant template written about any site but yours.

The second mistake: contact pages with dead links. A mistyped email, social links to deleted accounts, a form sending to an old address. Make checking these links a quarterly habit — they break silently. The third mistake: contradicting information between pages — an update date in the privacy policy older than the first article's date, or the site owner's name in About differing from the footer name. Small contradictions plant big doubt, and consistency builds the opposite with equal force.

The fourth mistake is specific to sites that build their pages right before applying: pages rushed overnight, without real phrasing, with links returning to the homepage emptily. Write these pages as part of building the site as we planned from the start of this series, and they will be natural and honest without strain. And the wrong assumption is that adding these pages after rejection cures it — it cures the cause if that was truly the cause, but the rejection stays in the site's record, and the wiser path is never to arrive there.

The fifth mistake is less obvious: pages that exist but are written in a second language different from the site's language, or in a design style that looks like another site. This happens often when pages are copied from a ready template or a previous project and their colors and fonts are never adjusted. For the visitor it is a break in the experience; for the reviewer it is a signal that the site is "assembled" from pieces its owner does not own. The final touch on every page is opening it and asking: does this page look like it was written here, in this site, by its owner?

8) The essential-pages checklist

The table below gathers everything above into a directly applicable form. Check your site item by item, and treat any unmet item as required work before applying — not optional polish:

PageWhat it must containCompletion mark
AboutClear identity, real experience, site's purpose, contact link and best articlesConsistent voice, face or personal logo
ContactDirect email + three-field form + honest status messageA test message actually reaches your inbox
Privacy PolicyData collected, its use, Google ads named with cookies, user options, update dateThe third-party ads paragraph exists in text
TermsContent usage rules, liability limits, comment rulesVisible update date at page top
DisclaimerEducational nature of content, not individual adviceAn honest sentence fitting your field
FooterAll above pages linked visibly on every site pageChecked from an article page, not just the homepage

Make this table a habit for every new site you start — the items will not change much between one site and another. If you use a ready platform, additions follow this same structure, and the principle is identical in all cases: complete, honest, reachable, consistent pages in language, name, and dates.

Before moving to the next part, take a useful habit: open your essential pages today and read them as a stranger reads them the first time. One line that feels generic — anything any site could write? Rewrite it with a detail specific to you. A broken link or old date? Fix it now before anyone sees it. This round takes under an hour, and it is the difference between a site that "meets the requirements" and a site where every page shows an owner who cares about details — an impression you win over competitors before anyone even discusses the content.
