The Road to AdSense (5): Domain and Hosting — What Actually Matters to Google and AdSense

27 October 202617 min read3128 words

After settling your niche in the previous part, it is time for the ground your site stands on: domain and hosting. This building layer looks technically complex to many, so they postpone it or hand it to videos comparing "the top five hosts" — while the truth is it consists of a few clear decisions once you understand what actually happens under the surface. In this article we unpack every one of these decisions: what it is, why it matters, and the right choice in different cases — without selling you a product or recommending a service for commission.

I will keep this part loyal to the series' rule: honesty first. I will not tell you the pricier domain brings better ranking — it does not — and I will not scare you with hosting problems that do not matter to a site starting with modest traffic. What you should leave this part with is three things: a domain name chosen with confidence, a clear understanding of your publishing options between free and paid, and a checklist of criteria to judge any host before paying it a single currency unit.

The rule of this part: Google does not care much who hosts your site — it cares what your visitor can do on it: open speed, stability, and a secure connection. Every decision here is measured by these three.

1) The domain: an identity living years, not an address forgotten

The domain is your site's address — the one the visitor types, saves, and revisits — and also the name that will appear in search results next to your titles for years. With that weight, a domain is not chosen in a rush nor from a list of available names the provider sorted by price. A good name approaches your niche, pronounces easily in your audience's language, needs no explanation when heard, and is spelled the way it sounds with no spelling ambiguity. These four traits are testable in minutes: say the name aloud to two people and ask them an hour later how they spell it.

Name length is a purely practical matter: short names of one or two words win at memory, typing, and verbal sharing, and every addition loses part of these advantages. Avoid names containing hyphens or numbers unless the number has fixed meaning in your brand name — the hyphen disappears in speech and the number confuses with its homophone letters. And if your audience is Arabic, many debate between an Arabic-script domain or a Latin spelling — the practical market-experience recommendation: a Latin name pronounceable in Arabic serves you in memory, sharing, and email together, and it is the choice most successful Arabic sites adopt.

Also know what does not matter in choosing a domain so you do not pay for market myths: having your niche's full keyword inside the name is not a ranking requirement, the domain's absolute age is not an advantage by itself, and a ".com" extension will not lift weak content above strong content. The name's value lives in human memory, not the indexing algorithm — and whoever understands this saves two months of hesitating between "the best SEO name" and simply picks a good name that is memorable, pronounceable, and lasting.

After the name comes the extension question. ".com" remains the most familiar and safest extension for memory, followed by country extensions like ".sa" and ".eg" when your geography is fixed, then the newer extensions fitting clear brands. Do not believe the extension directly raises or lowers your Google ranking — the effect is practical and behavioral: the visitor trusts what they know, and a familiar extension reduces doubt at the first click. Buy the name for several years from the start — it saves a little and prevents the domain-expiry catastrophe that drops whole sites out of indexing for weeks.

And before paying, a simple legal check saves you a big problem: confirm the name does not represent a registered trademark in your field, and does not approach a famous site's name closely enough to be accused of imitation. A Google search for the name between quotation marks reveals most conflicts, and moving later from a problematic name costs you the whole built identity. Also enable auto-renewal in your domain provider's account from day one, and add a yearly calendar reminder thirty days before renewal — two protection layers against the most repeated and costly site-owner mistake: forgetting renewal.

2) Subdomain or independent: the decision confusing beginners

This question repeats often because its answers are muddled: do you build your blog on a subdomain like blog.example.com or on an independent domain like example.com? The honest answer depends on your situation, and the difference is practical more than technical. The subdomain lives under an existing domain's umbrella — your main site, for example — benefiting from its established trust and saving the domain cost, but it remains subordinate: its reputation builds on the parent's, and if the parent is weak or topic-mismatched, the subdomain starts from weakness, not zero.

The independent domain gives you a full identity growing on its own: its ranking is unaffected by another site's problems, you build everything on it from scratch, and it offers complete flexibility in structure and migration. In exchange: the trivial yearly domain cost and starting trust from zero — a start every major site existing today has passed. The direct recommendation for your first profit-intended project: an independent domain for the blog, because it is the future-proof option — if the site succeeds and you later decide to sell, expand, or separate, you own a complete asset, while the subdomain remains tied to the parent domain's fate forever.

Legitimate subdomain cases exist and deserve mention: if you have a strong main site in the same field and add a blog as its natural extension, the subdomain is a logical choice keeping the brand one. And if you are building a first learning experiment and do not know whether you will take it far, starting on a free platform with a subdomain is legitimate practice — but know that later migration to an independent domain needs a careful conversion plan preserving links and indexing, an effort the one who starts correctly from day one spares himself.

3) Hosting: speed first, support second, price third

Hosting is the server where your site's files are stored and served on every visit. Its first and most important criterion: server response time — the time until the first byte of the site reaches you on request. Companies do not advertise this number because they are not equal in it, but it shows directly in Google's free tools: open the Core Web Vitals report in Search Console for a site on the host you consider, or test successful similar sites on that host. A slow server slows all your pages no matter how you optimize your code — the ground comes before the building.

After speed comes technical support, the criterion whose value appears only in your site's worst moments. Your site will go down once — all sites do — and the difference between losing a day and losing an hour is support quality, speed, and language. Test support before buying with a real pre-sales question: measure response time and answer clarity. Third criterion is declared uptime: ask for a documented number above 99.5 percent, and remember that "unlimited" in ads is usually commercial phrasing with hidden limits in the terms — read the allowed monthly visitor range in the contract before being dazzled by "no limits."

The forgotten fourth criterion: backups. Ask clearly before buying: does the host take automatic backups? Every two days or weekly? How long does restoration take if requested? Dynamic sites — WordPress specifically — need regular backups covering both files and the database, and losing a site without a backup compresses months of work into one error message. Add to the host's copies a monthly manual copy of yours in an external place — this habit costs minutes and saves your site at the moment all parties feel everything is under control.

As for price, it is a fourth criterion ranked after the three, not before them. A shared hosting plan is fully sufficient for a site starting with hundreds of daily visitors, and dedicated servers costing tens are unnecessary before demand calls for them. Yearly billing instead of monthly usually saves noticeably, but buy one year first, not several, until you actually test the service then renew if satisfied. And a field tip: stay away from offers pulling you into subscribing for extra services you do not understand at checkout — what you need at the start is one plan, one domain, and a security certificate.

4) HTTPS: one condition that is not negotiable

The secure HTTPS connection — the padlock next to the address — has not been optional for years: Google considers it an official ranking signal, Chrome and Firefox browsers warn visitors explicitly about lockless sites, and AdSense itself prefers secure sites. Practically: a site without HTTPS loses ranking, trust, and visitors at once, while enabling it today costs almost nothing — free Let's Encrypt certificates come with most hosts in a single activation step from the control panel.

More important than enabling is completeness: a site is "secure" only if all its pages and all its files load over the encrypted connection. One page opening over http or one image pulled from an unencrypted link is enough to turn off the padlock and trigger warnings. After enabling, set the automatic redirect from http to https for all links, then open your site and navigate page by page confirming the padlock stays everywhere — this ten-minute round prevents a problem that would chase you for months if neglected.

A clarification ends a recurring question: you do not need a paid certificate with extra features for an ordinary content site. Free certificates encrypt the connection with exactly the same strength as paid encryption, and the differences in commercial certificates concern large corporations needing institutional verified identity inside the certificate itself. Your site — a content blog — suffices with the free green padlock renewing automatically every three months; whoever tries to sell you an expensive yearly certificate "for your new site" is selling what you do not need through safety feelings, not technical truth.

And if your site is brand new, there is no issue at all: set HTTPS from the first moment, before the first article. Whoever migrates an existing site from http to https adds one extra step: updating the preferred domain version in Search Console afterward, because Google treats the two versions as two addresses until you tell it about the redirect. The details exist step by step in the first Search Console steps article on this blog — what matters here is putting security on the foundations list, not the "later improvements" list.

5) DNS: two minutes to understand what happens

The term DNS scares non-technical people though it is simpler than it looks: it is the address book translating the human-meaningful domain name — example.com — into a computer-understandable server address. When you type a site address in the browser, the system asks this book: where does this name live? Then it goes to the answer. Everything you do connecting a domain to a host, email, or a pages service is editing one or two lines in this book through the domain provider's panel.

The entries you will deal with are limited types: an A record linking the name to a direct server, a CNAME record linking it to another name — what cloud publishing services use — plus MX records for email and TXT records for verifications. You do not need to become an expert in them; you need to understand that every service you add to your site hands you a ready line to paste in the right place, and that edits need time to propagate globally from minutes to hours — so do not panic if the site does not appear immediately after linking; that is the system's normal behavior.

One practical lesson worth saving from this section: stay the owner of your domain at a trusted provider and separate the domain decision from the hosting decision. Many buy both from one company then find themselves locked when they want to change hosts: transferring a domain between providers is a possible but slow procedure, while editing DNS to point the site to a new host takes minutes. Domain at a large specialized provider, hosting with whoever excels in speed and support — connecting the two through the DNS lines you now understand.

And because manual editing in DNS records is the most repeated publishing-error source, follow three protective rules: copy the new record exactly as the service gave it — one letter's difference breaks the link; do not delete an existing record before the new one proves it works; and document every edit with its date and reason in a simple file. These three rules compress the hours of confusion beginners spend asking "why doesn't my site appear after linking?" while the answer is a mistyped letter in a record written from memory instead of copied.

6) Free publishing options: when they suffice and when not

Before buying hosting, know there is a serious, fully free option: static publishing services like GitHub Pages, Netlify, and Cloudflare Pages host your site at no cost with excellent speed and automatic security certificates. These services have one condition: your site is ready pages — HTML, CSS, and JavaScript — with no content management system generating pages on request. Every blog built on this series' methodology can be published on these services as is, and this is the best option for a site starting on a zero budget that writes its content in files.

The decision map is thus clear: a files site without WordPress → free static publishing services, which suffice for real beginnings and handle large traffic. A WordPress or dynamic site → paid hosting, because these systems need a server executing their software. A site starting free then growing → static services too, because upgrading later is easy and cheap compared to moving a site from a weak host to another. The mistake many make is buying expensive hosting for a site needing static pages, or the reverse: building a heavy dynamic site because they did not know the lighter alternative.

And do not forget the maintenance time this equation demands: the dynamic system needs periodic core and plugin updates and backups you are responsible for, while the static site is nearly unhackable and needs no updating because no software runs in it. This hidden cost of time and worry is a real reason to prefer static building for content projects, and it is the same philosophy of this blog — built entirely this way and published on a free service with speed rivaling paid sites.

And a question logically follows this section: what if you start on one option then need to move to another? The path exists in every direction at different costs. From paid hosting to a static service requires rebuilding the site as ready pages — big effort for a content-rich WordPress site.

From a static service to paid hosting or another static service is much easier: copy the files, edit the two DNS records you understood in the previous section, and wait for propagation. This essential difference in movability is one more reason — after cost, speed, and security — to prefer the static site as the start of a serious content project.

Save this table with the pages checklist from part three — together they cover the technical and credibility layer everything remaining in the series stands on. The decisions in this table do not change much between projects, so take them once and print them in your memory for every coming site.

And one last thing worth saying clearly: do not let this part delay your start. A common mistake is the beginner spending a week comparing hosts, reading hundreds of reviews, and changing the decision three times — while the real differences between reasonable options are far smaller than the value of a week of content writing. A good decision today — a logical name, a publishing option fitting your case, security enabled — beats the perfect decision never taken. Start with what you have, apply the checklist later on what you built, for everything in this layer can improve without demolition.
