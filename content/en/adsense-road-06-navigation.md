With the foundations complete in the previous five parts — niche, pages, domain, hosting — we reach the layer the visitor and crawler face first upon entering: the navigation structure. This layer looks simple so it gets neglected, but it is actually your strongest tool for guiding the reader from page to page and telling Google how your pages relate to each other. A badly navigated site loses the visitor who feels lost, loses the crawler that does not understand your priorities, and loses the review team that finds a site resembling a store with no shelves.

The difference between a successful and a failed navigation structure is not measured by menu beauty or dropdown animations — it is measured by two questions only: can a new visitor find what they came searching for in three clicks or fewer? And does it show them clearly what they should read next? Everything we apply in this part serves these two questions, from organizing categories to the shape of links inside article texts themselves.

> The rule of this part: navigation is not a tree of headings arranged for arrangement's sake — it is decision paths taking the visitor from "I don't know" to "found it" then "I want more" with the least possible friction.

## 1) The three-clicks rule: reasonable depth, not full depth

The famous usability rule says: any page on your site should be at most three clicks away from the homepage. It is not a law written in any policy, but it is a summary of real human behavior: every extra click loses a percentage of visitors, and after the third the surrender rate clearly rises. For the average blog this rule is easy to verify: Home → Category → Article verifies in two clicks, and the legal pages in the footer are one click from anywhere.

The problem starts when archives grow: a hundred articles in one category mean numbered archive pages where deep articles become hard to reach. The solution is not splitting categories into subcategories and sub-subcategories — that complicates the tree — but building internal links inside the articles themselves so old articles point to new ones and vice versa, forming a link network that completes what the category tree alone cannot. We will build this in detail in the internal links section below.

And remember the three-clicks rule is measured from the visitor's viewpoint, not your file structure. A "more" link opening an intermediate page then a list page then the article is three meaningless clicks — the menu should open the article directly. Check your site yourself from the homepage to your deepest page, and delete every intermediate page not serving a real decision — this round reveals to you what bothers your visitors daily without them telling you.

And an element helping this shallow depth is the breadcrumb: the small line above the article showing "Home ← Category ← Article title." This line serves three parties at once: the new visitor sees where they are on the site map at a glance, the deep visitor steps back one level in a single click, and the crawler reads the section structure from inside every page. Google sometimes displays the breadcrumb in the search results themselves instead of the long URL — free extra visibility for a small code spot.

 If your theme does not show it, it is among the first improvements worth adding; one line of structured data is enough for Google to understand it. Most modern themes support it with a single setting, and the reward is a cleaner, richer presence in search results.

But watch a common confusion that spoils this element: the breadcrumb reflects the real section structure, not the specific visitor's click path. A visitor arriving from search directly into the article did not "pass" by the category at all — yet the breadcrumb shows it because it is their logical place in the tree. Whoever mixes the two paths builds dynamic, changing breadcrumbs that harm reader and crawler alike, while the fixed path built on sections stays clear and reliable in all cases.

## 2) The category system: sections living years without chaos

Categories are the backbone of blog navigation, and building them wrong accumulates as content grows until they become an obstacle. First rule: a small limited number — five to seven categories suffice a medium blog however it grows, because the visitor memorizes seven sections and not twenty. Second rule: categories are equal-level and non-overlapping — "WordPress" and "SEO" are two parallel categories, while "SEO" and "link building" is confusing overlap: where does an article about SEO links go?

The most important third rule: every category needs a clear content future. Before opening a new category ask: can I write ten articles in it now? A category with two articles looks like an abandoned list, and an abandoned list suggests the site itself is abandoned. Start with the categories you have immediate stock for, and add the new one when content presses against it. This triple rule — limited number, no overlap, guaranteed future — is all you need for a category system that survives.

And in bilingual blogs like this one, add a fourth rule: the same categories in both languages, with the same identifiers and translated names. The reader moving from the Arabic version to the English one should find the same map, not a second invented system. This consistency serves the bilingual visitor and the crawler understanding the versions' relationship, and it is among the details making a bilingual site look like one project, not two conditional sites.

And naming categories itself is a small science worth two minutes: the category name describes its content in the reader's language, not in celebratory internal language — "AdSense & Google earnings" is clearer than "gains," and "SEO & indexing" clearer than "visibility." Short descriptive names are understood from the first time, appear in category pages and their titles which may themselves get indexed, turning the category name into an extra keyword phrase for the whole site. And fix the names from the start, avoiding repeated renaming — every rename means updating the category page, its internal links, and its reference in every dependent article.

## 3) The main menu: little enters, much stays

The main menu at the top is the most valuable real estate in your navigation: every element in it appears on every page, and every extra element distracts from the rest. The proven blog formula: Home, All articles, Categories (dropdown), About, and one main button for the commercial goal — "Request a service" for a site offering services. Five elements suffice and deliver, and every addition beyond that turns the menu into a crowded market losing its guiding function.

The categories dropdown is better than displaying them horizontally in five columns, because it keeps the menu clean and opens the full map on demand. And make sure it is usable on mobile — dropdowns relying on mouse hover do not work by touch, so they must open by tap. This detail is among the things tested in the first real mobile use, and among the marks of a site whose owner actually tried it on their own device.

And do not put in the main menu what the visitor decides nothing from: archive links detailed by year, the tags page, external links — all belong to the footer or internal pages. A practical menu test: ask someone who never visited your site to find something specific in ten seconds while you watch without helping. If they hesitate or take the wrong path, the menu needs review before any other site improvement.

And the mobile menu deserves independent review from its desktop counterpart, because it receives today more than half of most blogs' visitors. The globally accepted form: a burger button opening a full-screen or dropdown menu, with large touch targets — at least forty-five pixels height per element — and spacing preventing accidental presses. And test the menu on a real device, not just a shrunk browser window, for touch movement and touch error are entirely different behavior from the mouse.

 An excellent desktop menu turns into a field of wrong taps on mobile if not adjusted for it, and that is among the most dangerous things harming site experience today without the owner feeling it. Borrow a phone from someone with a different screen size than yours and watch them use the menu once.

## 4) Internal links inside the article: the network making the difference

Internal links inside article texts are your strongest navigation tool and the most neglected. The dual function is clear: the reader finishing a paragraph needing depth finds a ready link taking them to the specialized article, and the crawler reading the link understands your page relates to its topic and distributes page value across the network. A blog without internal links resembles a building with no doors between rooms: every visitor sees one room then exits.

The good internal link formula has rules: the link text describes its destination — "your first Search Console steps guide" — not empty phrases like "read here" or "click here," because link text is a real SEO signal not to be wasted. And the link is placed where the reader actually needs depth, not at the end of every circumstantial sentence. Three to six internal links per long article is a sane number balancing benefit and distraction.

And the most important habit in this file: the bidirectional link. Whenever you link a new article to an old one, go back to the old one and add a link to the new one in a suitable place. This habit builds a mutually supporting network instead of a single line, letting every new article benefit from the indexed old articles' trust, and vice versa. The generator assembling our blog verifies part linkage automatically, but contextual links inside text need your hand — ten minutes per new article returning to three old articles to update their links.

And right after the article's end, the automatic links layer is added: a "related posts" box showing three articles from the same category or series. This box is built once in the template then works automatically with every new article, and it is the highest-effectiveness spot for retaining the reader after the closing line — the moment the visitor usually decides to leave. The good box shows title, excerpt, and a small cover, and does not depend on heavy additions slowing the page for a function buildable in a few lines, as applied in this very blog.

## 5) The footer: the complete reference map

The footer is not a formal tail — it is the natural place for reference pages and fixed links: About, Contact, Privacy Policy, Terms, Disclosure, and the blog's sections map. The regular visitor and the reviewer alike know the footer is where these pages live, and their absence from their expected place counts as absence even if they exist elsewhere. Hence the rule: the top menu for daily navigation, the footer for references and obligations — the two are not mixed.

A useful footer column especially for big blogs: latest posts or most read. The visitor reaching the page bottom has read something and approached the end, and offering three suggested links at exactly this moment is the highest available chance to retain them one more minute. This is not an inflation trick — it is a logical continuation of a reading journey that finished its text but not the reader's need.

And keep the footer consistent across all pages — the same links in the same order on homepage, articles, and static pages. The occasional disappearance of a link from one page but not another looks small but suggests a site built from incomplete templates. A site where all its fixed elements appear on all its pages without exception suggests the exact opposite: one studied structure respecting its visitor wherever they land.

A last footer detail deserves a conscious decision: social media links. The golden rule here is the same social rule everywhere: do not put icons for dead or semi-dead accounts. A Twitter account whose last tweet is two years old is an advertisement for neglect, while an active daily Telegram account is a living contact channel and a real distribution medium. Open a place for every account you actually maintain, and delete the rest without grief — the short living footer beats the crowded dead one, the same principle we applied to the top menu applying here with its full letters.

## 6) Search and archive: the emergency doors

Internal search in a blog is a small-to-implement, big-impact feature: the visitor who came with a specific intent and does not see it in the menus types it directly into search. Good implementation opens a quick panel, searches titles and content instantly, and shows results with the matching word highlighted. If your platform has no built-in search, a simple solution serves: a Google search link scoped to your domain — not ideal, but better than nothing and serving the immediate visitor.

The full archive — a page showing all articles sorted by date or category — is the second layer of these doors. Some visitors prefer browsing over searching, and the crawler benefits from a page linking all articles with direct links. A good archive displays the article with its date and category and allows filtering by section, turning from a flat list into a real exploration tool.

And remember the search and archive pages are among the pages where ads are not placed, as we will detail later in the policies part — their function is guidance, not monetization. A search results page with ads suggests the results themselves are ads, a confusion against the policies' spirit and harmful to trust. Keep these pages clean, purely functional — among the details Google reviews catch in evaluating the experience's quality as a whole.

And there is a hidden gain from internal search most bloggers overlook: its own data is a content-ideas source. What do your site's visitors search inside its search box and not find? Every search phrase with insufficient results — or unconvincing ones — is a real question from a real reader who reached your door themselves then found nothing satisfying their need. This list is more honest than any external keyword tool, because it is data from your own audience.

 Make reviewing internal search logs a monthly habit, and you will find your coming article titles there, demand-confirmed and ready — a perfect bridge to what we will build in the coming publishing parts. No external tool can give you this certainty, because this data comes from people who already chose your site.

## 7) The navigation checklist table

The table below gathers this part's criteria into one check. Apply it to your site from a new visitor's viewpoint, not the owner's who memorized all paths: Every row below is explained in a previous section of this part, with the reasoning and the practical steps.

| Criterion | Check | Success sign |
|-----------|-------|---------------|
| Three clicks | Any page opens within three clicks of home | No purposeless intermediate pages |
| Categories | Five to seven non-overlapping sections, ten potential articles each | Every section active, not decoration |
| Main menu | Five items or fewer + one clear goal button | Passes the ten-seconds test |
| Internal links | Three to six descriptive links per long article | A bidirectional network, not a single line |
| Footer | All legal pages + blog sections on every page | Fully consistent, no exceptions |
| Search and archive | Instant search and a filterable full archive | Clean, ad-free |

This table is among the lists checked once and revisited at every big site addition. A new category, a modified menu, a new article type — all are moments to return to this table confirming the network did not spoil with growth, for an unrevised structure quietly decays while its owner keeps believing it is fine.

And do not run this check alone as the site's owner — one human memorizing all paths does not test them. Familiarity is the enemy of testing: what is obvious to you after months of building may be a maze to someone landing for the first time.

 Ask two people from outside the project to perform two specific tasks: find a specific-topic article, and reach the privacy policy from a deep article page. Watch the clicks without guidance, and notice where hesitation stops them. Most surprises in this experiment come from places you believed obvious because you designed them: a menu item whose name does not say what it says to you, or a category you expected to stay clear needing a clearer title. Twenty minutes watching real visitors saves months of believing everything is in place.
