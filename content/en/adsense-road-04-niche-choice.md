Niche choice is the one decision in site building that cannot be easily corrected later. Hosting changes in an hour, a theme is replaced in a day, and a privacy policy is written in one evening — but if you choose the niche wrong, everything you build on top stands on wrong ground: articles nobody reads, keywords with no searchers, or a topic where advertisers pay pennies so your site dies economically before it lives. In this part we put a frame of three sequential tests around this decision, instead of guessing and enthusiasm.

The frame you are about to walk through is not theoretical; it is the same order I used in every project with a different ending: the idea that passes all three tests gets built, and the idea that fails one test gets rejected no matter how attractive it looks. This austerity is what protects you from this field's most famous trap: discovering after sixty articles that your topic has no searching audience, or has an audience but no advertisers willing to pay. One hour of testing before building saves you months of work in the wrong direction.

> The rule of this part: the right niche passes all three tests together — your actual knowledge, searcher demand, and advertiser interest — and any two out of three are not enough.

## 1) The knowledge test: do you know the topic or know about it

The first question sounds simple but drops most ideas: have you experienced this topic yourself, or only read about it? The difference is what Google's evaluation systems capture under the name of first-hand experience, and it is also the difference a reader touches within the first two paragraphs. Whoever tried writes details never written before: real numbers, real mistakes, tested step orders. Whoever only read remixes others' ideas in new phrasing — and that content thousands of sites already provided with no added value.

To turn this question into a measurable test, write on paper ten things you know about the topic that you did not read in one specific article, but learned from practice. Ten points easily? You have enough experience capital to build a whole site. Five shaky points? Your experience is partial, and the topic needs narrowing to an angle you know well instead of the whole subject. One or two points? This is not your niche — no matter how big its audience or how tempting its money, you will end up rewriting what others wrote, which is the most common rejection type in AdSense today.

Watch out for a common companion trap: the hobby you love may be your real experience even if it does not look "professional." Someone cooking for their family daily for ten years has real applied experience; someone fixing their own devices since their teens likewise. Do not belittle hobby experience next to job experience. The criterion is that you were the user who solved problems yourself, not the reader watching — and that criterion applies to thousands of topics you may consider "unworthy" while they are honest content mines only their owners can fill.

To settle this test objectively, rely on a simple, effective tool: the experience log. Open a file and write — across a week, without forced effort — every problem in your field you solve or help solve, with its details: what you tried, what failed, what finally worked. If the file fills with twenty real interventions in one week, you live inside the niche, not on its edge. If you notice after a week that the log is empty without effort, that is a clear answer before you spend months discovering it the expensive way: after publishing.

## 2) The demand test: does anyone actually search

Knowledge without search demand builds an excellent site nobody reads. The second test measures the audience's existence before you write a letter for them, and can be executed with free tools alone — no paid subscriptions. Start with the Google search box: type the beginning of a phrase from your field and watch the autocomplete suggestions — that is real search data from real users. Then scroll to the bottom of results and read "related searches." Then open any result in your field and watch the "People also ask" box — every question in it is a potential article title with proven demand.

Right after Google comes the communities test: the Facebook groups, Reddit communities, and Telegram channels specialized in your field. Read the last hundred questions posted and watch the repetition: questions asked weekly that keep getting incomplete answers are exactly what the audience searches for and does not find. Collect these questions in a table, and the result will surprise you: in every real niche there are dozens of recurring questions with no complete Arabic reference answering them. This list is the seed of your first content plan — actually demanded, not invented.

The fine numbers — monthly search volume per keyword — are useful but not a start condition. Their free tools suffice for estimating the general trend, and what matters at this stage is the pattern's existence, not the number's precision: a field with continuously renewing questions, multiple suggestions, and active communities is a living field needing no numeric certificate. But a field you penetrate search in without suggestions or renewing questions has a clear signature: a limited audience that drains quickly no matter how well you write.

Also watch demand seasonality, a factor beginners overlook and end up in niches thriving two months a year. Some topics — exams, seasons, annual reports — have a clear peak then a sharp drop. Seasonality is not a flaw in itself, but you must know it before committing so you do not interpret low certain months as a failure of your plan. The ideal beginner niche has steady baseline demand all year; if it has a seasonal peak on top, that is a bonus, not a backbone. Test it yourself: write your field's top ten phrases and notice which months keep appearing in suggestions and titles.

And the third layer of the demand test is read from the competitors themselves: examine the Arabic sites writing in the field and determine whether they are alive or abandoned. An active site publishing monthly and gathering comments and shares means a living market that can hold a new site.

 But if everything you found is an archive stopped years ago, examine the reason before judging: if the topic itself died, stay away; if results are poorly written despite a living topic, you face the best possible case — a real quality gap needing only a competent filler. The difference between the two cases shows in the current audience's engagement, not in publishing dates alone.

## 3) The advertiser test: who pays in this field

AdSense sells ad spaces in an auction, and that auction's value differs radically between fields. A field where banks, software companies, and insurers compete for ad display pays per click multiples of what a field with only small merchants pays. That is why an excellent site fails to make any income: its audience exists, its content is good, but nobody bids on its spaces. The third test examines this before you invest months of writing.

The practical way to check without paid tools: search Google for core phrases from your field and watch what appears at the top of the page and between results. Many fields show direct ads on search — that is a sign of paying advertisers. Also examine the big sites in the same field: if they carry major-network ads, the field carries advertising. And notice the kind of products that can be advertised at all: a field with paid, repeatedly used tools and services has a deeper ad market than a field where one purchase happens once in a lifetime.

But this test does not work alone — here lies the most famous trap: fields where an individual writer cannot possibly write with real experience, yet whose advertisers are the richest of all — insurance, finance, and investing are the classic example.

 If you land on a field that pays much and you do not truly know, you face a failing test despite its financial temptation, because the high auction will not rescue content lacking experience. The pattern repeats endlessly in the market, and recognizing it in advance is half the protection. The correct equation: always start from the list of what you know, then rank it by demand and advertiser interest, and pick the top — never start from the list of the richest fields and then search for what you know inside them.

Setting your financial expectations on realistic ground before committing protects you from the disappointment of the first months that drops many sites that were on their way to success. The high auction value does not mean you get your share of it — it enters an equation with the network's share, impression quality, the visitor's geography, and behavior. The number you can actually influence is the type of content that attracts an audience ready to buy, not a passing curious audience — and that is the practical meaning of "choose a niche advertisers like": not magic words in titles, but a reader representing a real opportunity for whoever pays.

And to complete the income picture in this test with an honest eye: AdSense is not the only income source in any niche, but it is the best test of its monetizability. A niche fit for Google ads — original content, real demand, existing advertisers — usually also fits complementary income: affiliate marketing for the field's products, service pages if you offer a service, sponsored content once you reach sufficient trust. The key is not to build the decision on a double untested promise: first test that the auction is alive in your field through AdSense, then add the other layers later as additions, not conditions.

## 4) Broad vs narrow: the size decision

After the three tests pass, the size question appears: a broad niche like "technology" or a narrow one like "task management tools for small teams"? The short answer for the beginner building their first site: narrow, with clear borders. A narrow niche lowers competition severity, makes every article serve its neighbor so internal links strengthen automatically, and makes the visitor who enters from one search find twenty more articles of the exact same interest. These three conditions together are what favor review and improve ranking at once.

The broad niche gives — theoretically — a bigger audience, but practically puts you in a race with giant sites and decades of archiving, and scatters your content across several alleys that do not support each other. The opposite mistake exists too: a niche so narrow it dries up, with ten topics only, then what? The practical standard I recommend: being able to write fifty genuinely different titles in the niche without repetition and without expanding the root topic. Fifty titles give you a full year of publishing floor, after which the field usually grows with you as your audience grows.

Do not run the fifty-titles test theoretically on one rushed sheet — run it in a focused session of at least two hours. One rushed mental pass produces a list of wishes; a real session produces a list of titles you could start writing tomorrow morning.

 Divide the sheet into your field's demand types: how-to guides, comparisons and tools, specific problem solutions, experiences and reviews, terms to explain. The right niche gives you titles in every one of the five boxes — a natural variety protecting your archive from monotony and covering different stages of the reader's journey. A niche giving titles in one box only — all "what is" for example — will feel its low ceiling after the first twenty articles; better to know that ceiling after passing it than to discover it mid-road.

For borderline cases there is a practical solution: the parent niche as an umbrella, the narrow angle as a start. Someone who knows technology broadly and cares about office tools starts a site specialized in office tools specifically, and when the archive completes and indexing stabilizes, expands cautiously into the broader umbrella through new interlinked sections. This gradual expansion preserves the first specialization's power and opens the horizon later, and it is farther from danger than starting broad immediately, which leaves the site with an identity the search engine cannot understand.

And the language question is a complementary decision to this test worth a stop: does your audience write in Arabic, or does the same opportunity exist in parallel English content? English phrase competition is generally harsher, but its ad auction is deeper; Arabic has real gaps in many specializations and an auction improving year after year.

 The beginner's practical decision: build in your strongest language first, where you write with faster and truer experience, and do not open the second version until the first archive matures — chasing both at the start ends both. This is the same methodology this blog follows: a complete Arabic archive with an English pair for every article, not two competing sites.

## 5) Analytical examples: real niche decisions

Let us apply the frame to three hypothetical but very typical cases. Case one: a front-end developer working in the field for years, thinking of the niche "programming tutorials for beginners." The tests: excellent real knowledge, huge demand, good advertiser interest. But competition is the problem: this is among the most complete fields in both Arabic and English. The solution is not abandoning the idea but narrowing it: "building performance-fast interfaces" or "practical database problem solutions" — angles where practical capital has a real edge over general copied reviews.

Case two: a language teacher who knows WordPress well, thinking of "digital teaching tools for educators." The tests pass: field experience, renewing demand because tools keep changing, medium advertiser interest from tool and hosting companies. This is a model of an ideal niche that is not superficially shiny: neither the richest nor the most famous, but its owner can write content nobody but practitioners can write, and actual competition is weak. Most genuinely profitable niches look like this: unglamorous in headlines, strong in auction and demand together.

Case three is the full trap pattern: an employee hears that "the investing field pays the highest per click" and decides to write an investing site with one month of knowledge. Demand is great and advertisers are the richest, but the knowledge test falls decisively. The expected result is known: rewriting what others publish, content without an original angle, and an AdSense rejection with the "low-value content" phrase. The rule drawn from the three cases is one: the order starts from your own capital, and money comes later behind experience — never the reverse.

The fourth case is a counter-pattern worth quoting: a professional content writer asks "what's the most useful niche to write about?" while practicing no technical or specialized field personally. Their only experience is writing itself — which is not a niche if the writer serves clients across twenty different fields.

 The practical solution here is not choosing a niche from outside, but narrowing from inside: look at your three most recurring client types across your working years, and pick their field where you have the deepest understanding of the questions clients pay to solve. The writer who served restaurants, stores, and clinics for five years owns three ready niches to test on this frame — far better than choosing "personal finance" because someone said it is the richest.

## 6) The niche decision table

The table below summarizes the whole frame into one applicable decision. Run every niche idea you consider through the rows, and commit only to what passes the first three rows together: Keep the answers concrete — numbers, examples, named advertisers — because vague answers are failed answers wearing nicer clothes.

| Test | Question | Success sign | Failure sign |
|------|----------|--------------|--------------|
| Real experience | Have you used and tried the topic yourself? | Ten experience points written instantly | You know the topic from reading only |
| Search demand | Does the audience search and renew questions? | Suggestions, questions, active communities | No suggestions, no renewing questions |
| Advertiser interest | Do advertisers bid on its spaces? | Visible ads in field results | No products or services advertised |
| Archive size | Do fifty titles survive without repetition? | A title list written in one session | Ideas dry after ten |

The idea passing the four rows is your niche; every other idea goes into a postponed-ideas file — it might serve a second site after you master the first. If you stand before two passing ideas, pick the higher advertiser interest between them, because their fine demand difference will show over time while the auction difference is known from day one.

And so this table stays a working tool and not a sheet to read, make it a file step: open a spreadsheet, put every niche idea you consider as a row, the four tests as columns, and fill them with specific answers, not impressions. The idea translated into precise lines — fifteen experience points, ten recurring questions, three advertiser types — is an idea chosen with seriousness. The idea described with generic words like "I think demand is big" already failed the test. This same file will become your reference when expanding, so do not abandon it after the first session.
